Human Activity Recognition

Pythonscikit-learnTSFELPCALLM prompting

The idea

A phone in your pocket records tiny wobbles through its accelerometer. From those wobbles alone, can a model tell whether you're walking, taking the stairs, sitting, standing or lying down?

In the drawing, the raw signal is turned into features, and a decision tree asks a handful of yes/no questions until it lands on an activity. We then asked a large language model to do the same from a few examples, and compared the two.

My contributions

  • Explored the UCI-HAR accelerometer data and visualised the six activities with PCA on total acceleration, on TSFEL features, and on the dataset's own features.
  • Trained decision trees on raw signals, TSFEL features and provided features, and compared them across tree depths.
  • Implemented a decision tree from scratch and compared its performance with scikit-learn's.
  • Classified activities with zero-shot and few-shot LLM prompting and compared it against the decision trees.

Next Word Predictor

PyTorchword embeddingst-SNEStreamlit

The idea

No labels needed. The model slides a window over War and Peace, looks up a learned vector for each word in the window, and a neural network guesses the word that comes next. Every sentence in the book is a free training example.

Along the way, words that behave alike drift close together in embedding space. The small scatter in the drawing is that idea, and we checked it with t-SNE.

My contributions

  • Developed a self-supervised text generation model trained on War and Peace that learns word embeddings.
  • Built a custom neural network for next-word prediction with adjustable hyperparameters such as context length and embedding size.
  • Visualised the learned embeddings with t-SNE to see which words cluster together.
  • Deployed a Streamlit app where you type a prompt, pick a model variant and generate text. It's live.

ML Models for Molecular Dynamics

PyTorchSchNetPacksGDML

The idea

Simulating molecules with quantum chemistry is accurate but painfully slow. SchNet and sGDML learn the potential energy surface from reference calculations instead.

The force on each atom is just the downhill slope of that surface: the red arrows in the drawing. Once the surface is learned, a simulation can take many cheap steps instead of a few expensive ones.

My contributions

  • Evaluated SchNet and sGDML pipelines for molecular force and energy prediction, reproducing and extending published experiments.
  • Wrote dataset conversion scripts (XYZ → NPZ) and built the database files that both training pipelines consume.
  • Set up training for force and energy prediction, and tuned hyperparameters like the number of interactions, basis functions and cutoff radius.
  • Evaluated the trained models with MSE and MAE.
